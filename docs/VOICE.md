# Voice

How the books sound. Read this before writing or editing prose. The goal: a
smart, friendly guide who has been where the reader is and wants them to get it.

## The tone

- Fun, warm, a little playful. Humor comes from the material and from honesty
  about how confusing things can be, never from forced jokes.
- Empathetic. Name the moment a reader is likely to feel lost ("this is where
  most people's eyes glaze over") and then get them through it. Never imply that
  something is obvious or easy.
- Speak to "you". Use "we" when working through something together. Use "I" for
  the author's own experience and opinions, sparingly and honestly.
- Confident and plain. If something is subtle, say so, then explain it. Don't
  hedge everything.

## Make technical things accessible

Explain in this order, and stop as soon as the reader has it:

1. The question the idea answers, in plain words.
2. A picture or a tiny example you can check by hand (five nodes, not five
   thousand).
3. The idea in words, with an analogy only if it really fits.
4. The formula, after the reader already knows what it says. Say what each
   symbol means, every time it matters.
5. The code, in the three-language tab block.
6. What the result means and what it does not.

Rules of thumb:

- Define a term the first time it appears, in a sentence, not a glossary entry.
- One new idea per paragraph. Short paragraphs. Vary sentence length.
- Prefer concrete over abstract: a named network of real people or things beats
  "a set of actors".
- Say why before how. Readers push through hard parts when they know the payoff.
- Be honest about limits, disagreements between methods, and common mistakes.
  These are the parts readers remember and trust.
- Prefer ordinary words ("use", "show", "start") to formal ones ("utilize",
  "demonstrate", "commence").

## Write like a person

- Specific beats generic. Replace a vague sentence with the example that made it
  true.
- Cut throat-clearing: openings that announce the topic, closings that repeat
  it, and stock transitions ("it is important to note that", "in today's
  world", "let's dive in", "in conclusion"). Start with the point.
- Don't stack lists of three by habit, and don't open every section the same way.
- Go easy on dashes and semicolons. Commas and periods do most jobs.
- Use contractions the way you would out loud ("it's", "don't", "you'll").
- Read it aloud. If you wouldn't say it to a student across a table, rewrite it.

## Sample

Flat:

> Degree centrality is defined as the number of ties incident to a node. It is
> a fundamental measure used to assess node importance.

In voice:

> Start with the simplest question you can ask about someone in a network: how
> many people are they connected to? That count is their degree. It's crude, and
> it's still the first thing worth checking, because a surprising number of
> bigger ideas turn out to be degree wearing a costume.

## Check before merging a chapter

- Could a motivated reader with no network background follow it?
- Is there at least one example they can verify by hand?
- Did a hard idea get a moment of reassurance and a payoff?
- Does it sound like one person talking, the same person as the other chapters?
